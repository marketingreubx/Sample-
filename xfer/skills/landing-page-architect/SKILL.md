# Landing Page Architect

A landing page fails for one of three reasons: the copy does not make a specific promise to a specific person, the layout makes the visitor work to understand it, or it looks like a template so it inherits zero trust. This skill fixes all three in one pass and outputs working code.

---

## RULE ZERO, NEVER FABRICATE

Never invent testimonials, client names, star ratings, customer counts, revenue figures, press logos, or case study results. These are the single most tempting things to fake on a landing page and the fastest way to destroy trust when a visitor checks.

When social proof is missing, do one of these:
1. Ask the user for their real numbers and quotes
2. Build the section with obvious placeholders, `[REAL TESTIMONIAL: name, role, specific result]`, never a plausible looking fake that could ship by accident
3. Replace the proof section with something honest that works without proof, a detailed process breakdown, a specific guarantee, or a founder note explaining why the offer exists

Never place logo bars of companies the user has no relationship with. "As seen in" bars with borrowed logos are the most common legal and trust failure on landing pages.

## NEVER USE EM DASHES OR EN DASHES

Not in copy, headlines, subheads, buttons, or code comments. Use a period, a comma, a colon, or restructure. Scan the final output for both characters before delivering. If either appears, rewrite that line.

---

## PART ONE, PICK THE PAGE TYPE FIRST

The two families need completely different structures. Choosing wrong is the most expensive mistake in this whole process, so decide before writing a word.

### Family A, direct response funnel page
One offer, one action, no navigation, no exits. Used for VSL pages, webinar and workshop registration, tripwire and low ticket offers, lead magnets, and long form sales pages. Traffic is usually paid and cold.

Defining traits:
- Zero nav links in the header, or a logo only. Every link is an exit.
- One call to action, repeated many times down the page, always the same action.
- Longer copy. Cold traffic needs the full argument.
- Design serves reading. Clean, high contrast, generous type size, minimal decoration.
- The page answers objections in sequence rather than showcasing features.

### Family B, product or service marketing page
Multiple paths, navigation present, visitor may be warm or researching. Used for SaaS homepages, agency and service sites, portfolios, app landing pages. All 10 reference images the user supplied are this family.

Defining traits:
- Full navigation, multiple destinations.
- A primary CTA plus a secondary lower commitment CTA (Watch demo, See pricing, View work).
- Shorter copy per section, more visual demonstration.
- Product UI, screenshots, or work samples carry the persuasion.

**Decision rule:** if the traffic is paid and cold and there is one thing to sell, build Family A. If the visitor arrived to evaluate a company or product with several possible next steps, build Family B. When the user is unclear, ask which one, since the entire structure depends on it.

## PART TWO, THE COPY SYSTEM

Design cannot rescue weak copy. Write the copy first, always, then lay it out.

### The headline

The headline does most of the work on the entire page. It must communicate, in one line, who this is for and what specific outcome they get.

**Three headline structures that work:**

1. **Outcome plus specificity.** State the result and the constraint that makes it credible. "Get 3 more consults a week without posting daily."
2. **The named mechanism.** Lead with the thing that makes your approach different. "The retainer reframe that moves estheticians from per post pricing to 3k months."
3. **The direct callout plus promise.** Name the reader, then the outcome. "For solo estheticians who are booked but not scaling."

**Headline rules:**
- Specific over clever, always. A clever headline that needs a second read has already lost.
- No abstract nouns as the subject. Solutions, journeys, experiences, and possibilities are not outcomes.
- Include a number, a timeframe, or a named constraint wherever honest.
- Under 12 words for Family B. Up to 20 acceptable for Family A where the argument needs more setup.
- The subhead adds the mechanism or the proof, it never restates the headline in different words.

### Section copy

**The rule the user asked for specifically: no unnecessary section labels.** Do not put eyebrow pills or category headings above sections, things like FEATURES, WHY CHOOSE US, OUR SERVICES, HOW IT WORKS, TESTIMONIALS, ABOUT US. They appear in most templates and they are the fastest visual tell that a page was assembled rather than written.

Instead, let the section headline carry the meaning by saying something. Compare:

| Template pattern | Written pattern |
|---|---|
| Eyebrow: FEATURES, Heading: What We Offer | Heading: Everything runs from one dashboard, including the parts you usually pay a second tool for |
| Eyebrow: WHY CHOOSE US, Heading: Our Benefits | Heading: Most tools show you the data. This one tells you what to do about it. |
| Eyebrow: TESTIMONIALS, Heading: What Clients Say | Heading: Three agencies switched last quarter. Here is what changed. |

A section heading should be a sentence with a claim in it, not a label naming the section's function. The visitor already knows they are looking at features when they see feature cards.

**Body copy rules:**
- Sentences under 20 words, most under 12
- Contractions throughout
- Second person singular, "you", never "you guys" or "our customers" when addressing the reader
- One idea per block. If a paragraph has two ideas, split it.
- Every claim followed by the reason it is true, not by another claim
- Concrete nouns over abstract ones. "A 40 minute onboarding call" over "a seamless onboarding experience"
- Cut every sentence that would survive being deleted

### Button copy

Never "Submit", "Learn More", "Click Here", or "Get Started" alone. The button states what happens next, in the visitor's words, from their side of the transaction.

Good: "Send me the playbook", "Book my strategy call", "Start the free trial", "Watch the 12 minute breakdown", "Get the templates".

For Family A pages, the same button text repeats down the whole page. Changing it between sections creates doubt about whether the actions differ.

## PART THREE, PAGE STRUCTURE

### Family A, direct response structure

Sections in this order. Cut what does not apply, never reorder.

1. **Hero.** Headline, subhead, one CTA, and either a video or a single supporting visual. If there is a VSL, it sits directly under the headline and everything else waits below the fold.
2. **The problem, named precisely.** The visitor should recognize their own situation in specific detail. This is where cold traffic decides whether you understand them.
3. **The turn.** Why the usual approaches fail. This creates the gap the offer fills.
4. **The mechanism.** What makes this different, named. Without this, the offer is just another promise.
5. **What they get.** The concrete deliverables, stated plainly, each with what it does for them.
6. **Proof.** Real results, real testimonials, real screenshots. Skip entirely if none exist rather than faking it.
7. **Objection handling.** The three or four real reasons they would not buy, answered directly. Usually price, time, whether it works for their specific situation, and whether they have failed at similar things before.
8. **The offer and price.** Stated clearly, no games.
9. **Risk reversal.** The guarantee in one sentence.
10. **Final CTA.** Same action, restated with the reason to act now.

### Family B, marketing page structure

1. **Hero.** Headline, subhead, primary CTA, secondary CTA, and a product visual or a person with floating proof elements.
2. **Trust bar.** Real client or integration logos immediately under the hero. Omit if none are real.
3. **The core value section.** Three cards or a split layout showing what the product actually does. Show the interface, not icons alone.
4. **The differentiator.** One section, deeper, on the single thing competitors do not do. This is usually the highest value section on the page and is usually missing.
5. **Proof.** Stats, testimonials, or case studies, real only.
6. **How it works.** Three or four steps, sequential, each showing the actual product or process.
7. **Pricing,** if applicable. Three tiers maximum, middle tier visually emphasized.
8. **Final CTA.** Restated primary action with a low friction alternative.

**Universal layout rules:**
- The first screen must communicate what this is, who it is for, and what to do next, with no scrolling.
- Alternate section backgrounds to create rhythm, but no more than three background treatments on the whole page.
- Vary section layouts. Three identical three card grids stacked in a row is the second biggest template tell after eyebrow labels.
- Every section earns its place or gets cut. A shorter page that says something beats a long page that fills space.

## PART FOUR, TYPOGRAPHY

Typography is the fastest way to make a page look designed rather than generated, and the fastest way to make it look generic if defaulted.

**Never ship the default stack.** Inter for everything, or system-ui alone, reads as unstyled. Choose deliberately.

**Pairings that work, by page personality:**

| Personality | Headings | Body | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Bold, modern, high contrast | Archivo, Satoshi, or General Sans, heavy weights 700 to 900 | Inter or Satoshi, 400 to 500 | The look in most of the reference images |
| Editorial, premium, considered | Fraunces, Instrument Serif, or Playfair Display | Inter, Söhne, or Work Sans | Serif headline against clean sans body reads expensive |
| Technical, precise, product led | Space Grotesk or Geist | Inter or IBM Plex Sans | Good for developer and data products |
| Warm, human, service business | Bricolage Grotesque or Cabinet Grotesk | Karla or Work Sans | Softer, less corporate |
| Direct response, maximum readability | Inter or Source Sans, 600 to 800 for heads | Inter or Georgia for long body | Family A pages, readability beats personality |

**Type rules:**
- Two families maximum on a page. Three only when one is used exclusively for a single accent element.
- Hero headline between 48px and 88px on desktop, never below 32px on mobile.
- Body copy 16px minimum, 17px to 18px preferred for long form Family A pages.
- Line height 1.1 to 1.2 for large headings, 1.5 to 1.7 for body.
- Line length 60 to 75 characters for body copy. Full width paragraphs on a wide screen are unreadable and look unfinished.
- Set headings tight, with negative letter spacing around minus 0.02em at large sizes.
- Use weight and size for hierarchy, not color. Gray body text on white is a readability failure, not a design choice.

**Mixing fonts for emphasis,** as seen in reference image 1 and 9: setting one or two words of a headline in a contrasting family or an italic serif creates a focal point cheaply. Use once per page maximum, on the hero headline, or it stops reading as intentional.

## PART FIVE, COLOR AND CONTRAST

**The structure that works, and that every reference image uses:** a near neutral base, one accent color, and one deep tone for weight. Not a palette of five competing colors.

- **Base.** Off white or a very light tint, not pure white. Something like #FAFAF8, #F7F8F5, or #FBFAFF reads more considered than #FFFFFF.
- **Accent.** One color, used for CTAs, active states, and small emphasis moments only. When everything is accent colored, nothing is.
- **Deep tone.** A dark version of the accent or a near black, for high impact sections and footers. Reference images 4 and 5 use dark green hero sections against white bodies to strong effect.
- **Neutrals.** Three grays for text, borders, and muted backgrounds.

**Contrast requirements, non negotiable:**
- Body text against its background: minimum 4.5:1
- Large text, 24px and above: minimum 3:1
- Button text against button fill: minimum 4.5:1
- Never place body text on a photograph without a scrim, overlay, or solid panel behind it
- Light gray text on white, anything lighter than #6B7280 on #FFFFFF, fails and looks washed out. This is the single most common contrast mistake.

**Applying color to sections:** use the deep tone for one or two high impact moments, usually the hero or the final CTA, and keep the rest on the light base. Alternating dark and light every section creates visual noise.

## PART SIX, IMAGERY AND VISUAL ELEMENTS

**Sourcing images.** Use direct Unsplash or Pexels URLs so the page renders immediately without local assets.

Format: `https://images.unsplash.com/photo-[ID]?w=1200&q=80&auto=format&fit=crop`

Choose photos that show the actual context of the business, a real workspace, real hands on a real tool, a real environment. Avoid the obvious stock signals: staged handshakes, generic groups laughing around a laptop, isolated headsets on white, anything with visible stock watermark styling.

**The floating proof card pattern,** used in reference images 2, 3, 7, 8, and 10, is the highest value visual device in the set and worth using deliberately. A person or product image with two or three small cards overlapping its edges, each carrying one real piece of information: a balance, a rating, a count, a status. It creates the impression of a live product rather than a marketing photo.

Rules for it: two to three cards maximum, each with one data point, positioned overlapping the image edge rather than floating in empty space, and the data inside them must be real or clearly illustrative of the product rather than a fabricated customer statistic.

**Cut out people on color shapes,** as in images 2, 5, 7, and 10, reads more designed than a rectangular photo and is worth doing when a person image is used at all.

**Product UI beats icons.** When showing what something does, show the actual interface, dashboard, or output. Icon plus heading plus paragraph cards are the weakest way to communicate a feature and the most template looking.

**Restraint rules:**
- One decorative device per page, gradient glow, grid lines, blobs, or dot patterns, not several
- Shadows soft and low opacity, or skip them entirely
- Border radius consistent across every element on the page
- Real spacing rhythm, sections separated by 80px to 140px on desktop, not cramped

## PART SEVEN, THE ANTI TEMPLATE RULES

These are what separate a page that looks made from one that looks generated.

**Banned copy:** unlock, unleash, elevate, harness, leverage, supercharge, skyrocket, dominate, game changer, level up, transform your, seamless, robust, cutting edge, revolutionary, empower, streamline your workflow, take your business to the next level, in today's fast paced world, imagine a world where, we believe that, it's not just X, it's Y.

**Banned section labels:** FEATURES, WHY CHOOSE US, OUR SERVICES, HOW IT WORKS, TESTIMONIALS, ABOUT US, OUR PROCESS, WHAT WE DO, GET STARTED, as standalone eyebrow text above a heading.

**Banned structures:**
- Three feature cards with an icon, a two word heading, and one generic sentence, repeated for every section
- Every section using the same centered heading plus centered subhead plus grid layout
- A hero with a centered headline and a single gradient button and nothing else
- Stat rows where the numbers are round and unsourced, 100+, 500+, 1000+, with no context
- Placeholder testimonials with invented names
- Lorem ipsum anywhere in delivered output
- Perfectly symmetric layouts throughout the entire page

**Required for a page to not look generated:**
- At least two structurally different section layouts, split, grid, full bleed, offset, or stacked
- At least one asymmetric moment, an offset image, an overlapping element, an intentionally uneven column
- Copy that contains at least one detail only this specific business would know
- A visual element that is specific to this business rather than interchangeable with any other page

## PART EIGHT, BUILD PROCESS

1. **Establish the inputs.** Business, offer, price, audience, the one action the page should drive, real proof available, and any brand colors or fonts. Ask only for what is missing and genuinely needed.
2. **Choose the family,** A or B, and state which and why in one line.
3. **Write all copy first,** headline through final CTA, before touching layout. Copy determines structure, not the reverse.
4. **Choose the type pairing and the three color values,** stated explicitly.
5. **Lay out sections,** varying the structure between them.
6. **Build as a single HTML file,** all CSS in a style block, responsive via CSS Grid and Flexbox, mobile breakpoint at 768px. Google Fonts loaded by link tag. No build step, no external dependencies beyond fonts and image URLs.
7. **Run the audit below,** fix every failure, then deliver.

**Technical output requirements:**
- Single self contained HTML file
- Fully responsive, tested mentally at 375px, 768px, and 1440px
- Semantic HTML, real heading hierarchy, h1 used once
- Alt text on every image
- Buttons and links with visible hover and focus states
- No JavaScript unless the page genuinely needs it, and then vanilla only

- [ ] Zero eyebrow section labels

- [ ] Button copy states what happens next, never "Submit" or "Learn More"
- [ ] Zero banned words or phrases

## OUTPUT

Deliver the working HTML file, then three short lines maximum:
- Which family and why
- The type and color decisions made
- What real inputs are still needed, if any, as `Needs from you: [specific thing]`

No walkthrough of the sections, no explanation of design theory, no summary of what was built. The page speaks for itself and the user can see it.

---

## EDGE CASES

**User supplies reference pages or screenshots.** Extract the structural patterns, spacing rhythm, and visual devices rather than copying the layout wholesale. Name the two or three specific things being carried across so the user can confirm.

**User has no proof, no testimonials, no logos.** Build the honest version. Lead with mechanism, process detail, and a strong guarantee instead. Say in one line that real proof would lift this page more than any design change.

**User wants a long form sales page.** Family A, and let it run long. Cold traffic sales pages legitimately run thousands of words. Do not compress the argument to make the page look tidy.

**User gives brand colors that fail contrast.** Keep the brand color for large elements and accents, introduce a compliant darker variant for text and buttons, and say in one line why.

**User asks for a specific reference site's look.** Build in that direction using structure and visual language, never clone. Copying a specific site's layout and copy is both a legal risk and a positioning failure, since it makes the user look derivative to anyone who knows the reference.

**Offer itself is weak or unclear.** Say so before building. A well designed page for an unclear offer converts worse than a plain page for a sharp one, and design cannot fix a proposition problem.
